# Worksheet 2 — Attractiveness Map (Evaluating Market Opportunities)

> Source: EPFL MOOC "From lab to market" transcripts (Week 4), plus the Where-to-Play book draft and cheat sheet.

---

## Purpose (in one sentence)

Worksheet 2 evaluates **each individual market opportunity** from the opportunity set along **two dimensions** — **Potential** (value creation: how big is the opportunity) and **Challenge** (value capture: how hard is it to conquer) — and plots it as a point on the **Attractiveness Map**, making the most attractive option(s) visible in comparison.

---

## Key concepts

- **Attractiveness Map** — the middle step of the Market Opportunity Navigator. A two-axis chart: **Y-axis = Potential**, **X-axis = Challenge**. Each market opportunity is positioned as a post-it / sticky note.
- **Potential (value creation)** — "how BIG is this opportunity? Is it at all worth pursuing? And if so, how much value can we create if we choose this path?" Refers to the **opportunity in itself, regardless of your own capabilities**. Mountain analogy: the **height of the mountain**.
- **Challenge (value capture)** — "which obstacles lie ahead of us if we decide to pursue this option? What are our main risks? How difficult will it be to overcome them and conquer this opportunity?" Refers to **your own capabilities and the obstacles**. Mountain analogy: the **chance of conquering the summit** (not its height).
- **The 6 factors** — 3 Potential factors + 3 Challenge factors, each scored separately, then combined into one overall score per dimension.
- **Overall Potential / Overall Challenge** — the combined score per dimension; yields the Y- and X-coordinate on the map. **Not a linear/mathematical sum** — a qualitative combination.
- **Design-thinking mapping** — Worksheet 2 corresponds to Desirability/Viability/Feasibility: Compelling reason to buy → **Desirability**; Market volume + Economic viability → **Viability**; the 3 Challenge factors → **Feasibility**.
- **Worksheet 2B — "Where to Impact"** — optional extension for double-bottom-line ventures: assesses social/environmental **impact** in advance (3 dedicated factors), marked with heart symbols.

---

## Normative steps / rules (the core)

**R0 — One worksheet per opportunity.** "Use a separate worksheet for each market opportunity that you evaluate."

**R1 — Sequence: prep first, then scoring.** Before the actual scoring, learn/understand three things: (1) the **world of the customers**, (2) the **business environment** (value chain + competition), (3) the **key milestones on the road to implementation**. (See section "Scoring scale & prep".)

**R2 — Score each factor separately, then combine.** "We recommend that you evaluate each factor separately, and then combine your scores into the rating of the overall Potential." The same applies to Challenge.

**R3 — Qualitative, not mathematical.** "This is not an algorithm or hard science. It is a qualitative methodology." The overall score is **"not a perfect linear summation"**. The natural tendency (especially among engineers) to translate the scale into numbers 1–4 and average them is to be explicitly avoided — instead, use systematic analysis to understand the pros/cons and then rate qualitatively.

**R4 — The X on the scale is NOT the objective.** "Putting the X on the scale is not the main objective of this exercise. The main objective is that you understand the upsides and downsides of your opportunities." When the scoring is uncertain: bring evidence and data before running off.

**R5 — Weights allowed, but consistent.** Factors may be weighted differently, "in case you see the need" — but then **consistently across all market opportunities** with the same weights.
> Nuance on equal weighting: the guiding-questions document and the book emphasize that **Compelling reason to buy** is a *necessary condition* and is analyzed first — when it scores low, "it makes life difficult, regardless of the other factors." The factors are therefore not strictly equal.

**R6 — Consistent time horizon.** Scores depend on the chosen time horizon (short- vs. long-term, depending on your own growth goals). Whichever is chosen — apply it **consistently across all options**. "Keep the time horizon consistent."

**R7 — Score absolutely AND relatively.** "Should we score a market opportunity in its own right, or relative to the other options? The answer is 'both'." First evaluate each as a stand-alone option in absolute terms, then consider it relative to the other options and adjust if needed.
> Plugin note: before proposing scores for a new opportunity, compare against the already-scored sheets factor by factor. Identical labels on all six factors is a red flag to cross-check, not a result.

**R8 — Quick & dirty as the starting point.** "We always start with what we call a quick and dirty evaluation, just putting your thoughts on the paper." The yield: a **list of key assumptions** and action items. It is a **"breathing document"** — refine, revise, adapt.

**R9 — Flag uncertainty.** Next to each factor, set a small marker / a colour scheme for the **confidence/uncertainty level** (red = no evidence, orange = some evidence, green = significant evidence). This steers the learning journey.
> Plugin note: the factor-level colour is the team's call. Claude proposes one with a one-line reason and asks it as a separate question from the score. Evidence colours that research puts on individual claims are input, not the factor colour. Uncertainty belongs in the colour, not in a drift to "Mid".

**R10 — Agnostic & adaptable.** Worksheet 2 is "agnostic to the industry/company type". Factors may be refined or supplemented (e.g. for established firms, "alignment with long-term strategic vision") — as long as it stays **consistent across all options**.

**R11 — Communication tool.** Putting Worksheet 2 on the table enables discussion/debate and different perspectives. "It's a communication tool. It's a shared language." Don't score based on your own opinion alone.

**R12 — Reshaping allowed.** Worksheet 2 helps make opportunities **more attractive**: examine low-scored Potential factors / high-scored Challenge factors — can they be improved? (Raise Potential via re-segmentation / a sub-segment; lower Challenge via partnerships.)

**R13 — "Not attractive" is a good learning outcome.** If an opportunity turns out to be insufficiently attractive: don't be disappointed — better to understand it now than after investing "sweat, tears and money". Back to the drawing board: revise, refine, change or discard.

## The 6 factors (3 Potential + 3 Challenge) — each with verbatim guiding questions

> The following guiding questions are taken **verbatim** from the "Clarifying and guiding questions for worksheet #2" document and the book draft (Part 2_a). They are the heart of the worksheet.

### DIMENSION 1 — POTENTIAL (Y-axis, value creation)

> Guiding statement: "The Potential dimension refers to how BIG is this opportunity? Is it at all worth pursuing? And if so, how much value can we create if we choose this path? … the potential dimension refers to the opportunity in itself, regardless of your own capabilities to succeed in it."

#### Factor 1 — Compelling reason to buy
**Core question:** "Will someone really want our offer and be willing to pay for it?"
**Meaning/rule:** "If compelling reason to buy is low — it's simply a 'No-Go', as demand will not fly." A necessary condition; analyze it first.

Three guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- Is there a real unmet need?
> - Can we provide an effective solution to this need?
> - And can we address it (much) better than current solutions?"

Deeper questions per sub-element (verbatim):

**Is there a real unmet need?**
> "Who exactly has this need? How do they currently solve the problem? Do they make any effort to actually solve the problem? And Is it a 'nice to have' or a 'must have' offer?"
> (book adds:) "What exactly is the problem/ need/ job to be done? Is it functional, social, emotional or basic need? … Who will be your economic buyer? Your user? … What will change with our offer? What are its main benefits? … Make vs. buy possibility — can customers make it themselves?"

**Can you provide an effective solution?**
> "Can you fully address it, and provide your customers with a *whole solution*? What strengths do you have that allow you to address this need, or do this job well? And What weaknesses do you have that hinder you from addressing this need?"
> (book adds:) "Do you create other needs — which you can't address — for the customers that will use your solution?"

**Better than current solutions?**
> "What other solutions exist for your customers? … What are the upsides of your solution relative to others? Why would customers prefer your product? And, just as important — Why would customers *not* prefer your product? In other words — Are the advantages of your solution really meaningful for the customers?"

**Important to remember (verbatim):**
> "Note that the only way to score this factor is to look through the eyes of your customers. It's not what *you* think, it's what *they* think that counts — what they say, or even more importantly, what they do. So Validate your believes, *go out of the building* and talk about this market opportunity with as many potential customers as possible."

#### Factor 2 — Market volume
**Core question:** "How big is this market, now and in the near future?"

Two guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- What is the size of the current market?
> - How much is it expected to grow over time?"

**Current market size? (verbatim):**
> "How many customers need your solution and How many customers can actually use it or buy it? This is an initial filter to narrow down the 'universe' of all customers. How many units would these customers buy annually and what would be the estimated revenues per customer? Combine the figures to estimate the total market size, that is, what would be your expected annual revenues if you had 100% market share?"

**Expected market growth? (verbatim):**
> "Is this a mature market by now? Has it grown in the last two years? And How much do you expect the need, or the number of customers to grow in the next 2-5 years?"

**Methods & rules (verbatim):**
> "We recommend to breakdown the size of the market into two elements: units and Euros (dollars)."
> "You can estimate the size of a market in a top-down approach — narrowing down the universe of customers, or in a bottom up approach — often termed 'nose counting'."
> Steve Blank: three market types — "*existing, re-segmented and new*". Existing/re-segmented are easier to estimate; new markets are "'guesstimation' rather than an exact evaluation".
> "It is interesting to note that a market which is not significantly BIG may still be an interesting option, especially if it can serve as a stepping stone towards a larger market."
> TAM/SAM/SOM differentiation: Total Available Market vs. Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM, the focus area, compared across options) vs. Serviceable Obtainable Market.

> "Big market": the book and guiding-questions document say that what counts as "big" depends on the industry/goals; in a VC-tech-startup context, "around hundreds of billions of dollars". If you assign numbers to the scale (low/mid/high/super high), keep them **consistent across all options**.

> Plugin note: the anchor is relative to the venture's context (industry, goals, funding path); "hundreds of billions" is the VC-tech example, not a universal bar. Agree with the team what "big" means for this venture before scoring, so the anchor doesn't push every Market volume down.

#### Factor 3 — Economic viability
**Core question:** "Is it worthwhile from a business perspective to pursue this market?"
**Rule:** At this early stage, a detailed 3–5 year P&L plan is "typically not feasible because the required information is simply too vague". Instead, use three guiding questions.

Three guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- Do you have sizeable margins?
> - Are the customers well-funded enough to pay the price?
> - How sticky will customers be?"

**Margins / value vs. cost (verbatim):**
> "Try to estimate the price that customers will be willing to pay and the cost of your product or service? What are the expected margins? Are they expected to change or grow over time?"
> (book adds:) "What is the estimated acquisition cost per customer?"

**Customers' ability to pay (verbatim):**
> "Do they already have a budget to solve the problem that you are addressing with your offering? And Is there someone who is economically responsible for that?" (especially relevant in B2B)

**Customers' stickiness (verbatim):**
> "How frequent will customers use your solution or re-buy it? And How difficult will it be for them to switch to alternative solution?"

**Important (verbatim):** "talk with your 'economic buyers' to understand their perspective." The **economic buyer** is not necessarily the user — it is the person "who spends the money on your good". Ensure slack between cost and willingness to pay; look for comparable offers as a price signal.

### DIMENSION 2 — CHALLENGE (X-axis, value capture)

> Guiding statement: "each market opportunity … is not only associated with its own value creation Potential but also entails its very own value capture Challenge. … while the Potential was assessed by looking at the market opportunity in and of itself, this dimension examines your own capabilities to succeed in it, and the main obstacles that you may face."

#### Factor 4 — Implementation obstacles
**Core question:** "How difficult will it be for you to create and deliver your offer?"

Three guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- How difficult will it be for you to develop the product?
> - How difficult will it be for you to access the market?
> - How challenging will it be for you to raise funding for this option?"

**Product development difficulties? (verbatim):**
> "Which technological hurdles have to be overcome? Are there any user-interface or design challenges that you might face? And Are there any regulation requirements that you should comply with?"

**Sales and distribution / market access? (verbatim):**
> "What type of distribution channel is required to gain access to customers? Are there adequate channels in place? How much time and money will be needed to build the channel?"
> (book adds:) "Is it important to have multiple channels? How costly will it be to operate/utilize the distribution channel? Are there effective marketing channels already in place? How costly will it be to create awareness and interest in your offer?"

**Funding challenges? (verbatim):**
> "What amount of money do you need to raise until customers start paying you? And How difficult will it be for you to raise this amount?"

**Important (verbatim):**
> "Innovators – and especially entrepreneurs with a technological background — often give much thought to the challenges of creating the offering, but neglect the challenges of bringing it to the market. Delivering the offering may sometimes be much more difficult than creating it, so give it a careful attention."
> Estimate funding bottom-up (R&D and sales people, equipment, marketing/sales). Book categories: "less than 100,000 USD, less than 500,000 USD, less than 2,000,000 USD or more than that."

#### Factor 5 — Time to revenues
**Core question:** "How long will it take until you can generate cash flow through sales?"

Three guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- What is the estimated time for development?
> - Will we need to wait until the market is ready for our offer?
> - How long is the sale cycle expected to be?"

**Development time? (verbatim):**
> "Think about the major milestones that you should accomplish until the product is ready for the market: How long will it take you to accomplish each milestone? And What is the overall estimated time until the product can be ready?"

**Time between product and market readiness? (verbatim):**
> "After your product is ready, what else should happen until it can be launched? think about value chain elements, required infrastructure, complementary products, etc. How much time will this take? And as a result: Is there a gap in time between product readiness and market readiness?"

**Length of sale cycle? (verbatim):**
> "A complex and long sale cycle is mostly relevant for B2B organizations rather than B2C. Think about How many people will you need to meet and convince — to make a sale? Are there possible resistances or gate keepers? What is the estimated time to make a sale? And What is the estimated deployment time, once the deal was sealed?"

**Important (verbatim):**
> "time to revenues is tightly related to the implementation obstacles that we assessed before. However, they emphasize two different perspectives that are both important." And: "revenues are only one key element to measure success in adoption. If your startup has different success metrics — such as a certain level of traction — you can use it to estimate the required time for adoption."
> B2C: sales cycle typically insignificant; B2B: can take months to several years.

#### Factor 6 — External risk(s)
**Core question:** "What obstacles in your business environment can stand in your way?"

Three guiding questions (verbatim):
> "- How threatened are you by competition?
> - How dependent are you on other companies or players?
> - How susceptive are you to adoption barriers?"

**Competitive threat? (verbatim):**
> "Who are your current competitors and Who may become your competitor in the future? How strong are they? And most importantly, Do you have a sustainable competitive advantage over them?"
> (book adds:) "Are there entry barriers for new entrants? (Patents, regulations, network externalities, etc.) … Is this advantage sustainable? (unique, difficult to imitate, durable)"

**Third-party dependencies? (verbatim):**
> "Think about who else needs to innovate for your innovation to be successful? Which other ecosystem players could affect the adoption of your offering? And how dependent are you on other 3rd parties like policy makers and regulators?"

**Barriers to adoption? (verbatim):**
> "Are the customers receptive to innovations? Is your product compatible with existing ways of doing things? Existing norms, standards, and infrastructure? In short — can it easily be adopted?"
> (book adds:) "How complex is your product? Can it be tried before purchase?"

**Important (verbatim):**
> "while entering a fortified field is dangerous, the lack of competition may be equally worrisome – either the market opportunity you are envisioning is not for real or the lack of competition means that you will carry the burden of educating the market and supplying the complementary elements." The greater your advantage over the competition, the better (an "unfair" competitive advantage helps the sale).
> **Direct vs. indirect:** Direct competition sells the same thing to the same people; indirect competition offers a different service that could satisfy the same need. Tools: **Competitive Table** and **Perceptual Mapping**.

## Scoring scale & prep

### Scale
- **Qualitative scale: "Low → Super High".** The book explicitly names the endpoints "from 'Low' to 'Super High'". In examples, intermediate steps appear: *low, low-mid, mid, mid-high, high, super high*.
- **Potential = Y-axis, Challenge = X-axis.**
- **Combination is "not a perfect linear summation"** — qualitative, not an average of 1–4. Goal: pattern recognition, understanding the pros/cons. (See R3.)

- **Seven steps:** **Low · Low–Mid · Mid · Mid–High · High · High–Super High · Super High** — the four main levels plus a half-step for a mark that sits between two of them. The authors' filled worksheets place an X on the line between two levels (e.g. Inergio RVs, Overall Challenge on the Mid/High line), and the authors' web app uses the same seven overall steps.

### Prep — "Before Starting Your Evaluation" (homework before scoring)
Learn/understand three things:

1. **Learn about your customers' world** — "think like a fish". Value proposition, the pain addressed, why it is better than existing solutions, what customers value, relevant trends.
2. **Learn about your business environment** — understand the **value chain** of this market (the chain of players who together serve market demand): Where do I fit in? Who stands in my way? Who has an incentive to cooperate? Plus: Who might be the **competitors**, what do they offer, how do they react, do I have a strong (possibly "unfair") advantage?
3. **Understand your key milestones on the road to implementation** — your own resources/capabilities: What do I already have, what still needs to be developed (tech know-how, human capital, IP, allies/partners, funding)?

(Cheat sheet, compact: "Before scoring, learn customers, value chain/competitors, and key implementation milestones.")

## The 4 zones of the Attractiveness Map

Four quadrants, spanned by Potential (Y) × Challenge (X):

| Zone | Potential | Challenge | Meaning / rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gold Mine** | high | low | "Your dream comes true" — ideal, but rare. Has the characteristics of a **Primary Market Opportunity**. Results from a significant unmet need or unique know-how. |
| **Moon Shot** | high | high | "high risk and high returns simultaneously". Truly innovative offers. A possible Primary or long-term **Growth option**. Most interesting for some investors, *if* the team can master the challenges. |
| **Quick Win** | low | low | "low risk–low return". Limited but safe potential. A good **jump-start / stepping stone**, combinable with other options. |
| **Questionable** | low | high | "least desirable quadrant" — usually to be avoided. Keep it in mind, since conditions can change. Many ventures fail because they unknowingly pursue a questionable option. |

**Reading the zones:** the map's midline sits at **Mid–High**. Low, Low–Mid and Mid count as *low*; High, High–Super High and Super High count as *high*. So Mid Potential with High Challenge is plainly **Questionable**, not a boundary case. Only an overall score of exactly **Mid–High** puts an opportunity on the line between two zones (both at Mid–High: the centre of the map). Then name both neighbouring zones and let the team discuss what tips it — don't force one. This is a qualitative reading, not arithmetic: the upsides and downsides matter more than the exact cell. Sources: the authors' blank map labels Low · Mid on one half of each axis and High · Super High on the other; MOOC 7.2.1 (Ceal: "mid-potential and high-challenge … questionable quadrant"; mid-high / mid-high "right at the center"); book Flyability recap (mid-high Potential + high Challenge = "border line between the Moon Shot and the Questionable quarters").

**Learning from the map:**
- Analyze the **individual opportunity** (trade-offs, up-/downsides in itself).
- Analyze **the set** (relative positions, spread across quadrants — variety is desirable to balance the downsides of the Primary).
- **Choose the Preferred / Primary Market Opportunity** → the first step of the Agile Focus Strategy (Worksheet 3).
- **Reshape an opportunity** to reach a better position (see R12).

## Validating & learning / Using AI for market research

### Validating & learning
**Basic stance:** "don't fall in love with your initial assessment. … at the beginning it's a list of hypotheses." Move from assumptions to evidence-based assessment.

Procedure (near-verbatim):
1. **Write down the reasons** that led to each score.
2. **Mark confidence** via the colour scheme: **red** = no evidence at all, **orange** = some evidence, **green** = significant evidence. Red/orange statements = the **list of assumptions**.
3. **Assumption Map** (tool): two dimensions — X = **Evidence** ("Do you have observable evidence, qualitative or quantitative, to support your hypothesis"), Y = **Importance** ("which hypothesis, if proven wrong, will cause your idea to fail"). **Focus on the top right** (important + little evidence).
4. **Design the learning journey:** What to learn next? How to gather evidence?
5. **Start with the Compelling reason to buy** (= Desirability) — "the necessary condition for a business opportunity. It's your riskiest assumption."
6. **Validate or disprove** assumptions, adjust the scoring, reposition the opportunity on the map.

**Reshaping:** Potential too low → raise it (e.g. re-segmentation, focus on a needier sub-segment). Challenges too high → lower them (e.g. partnerships). "It's a learning journey … a breathing document."

### Using AI for market research
**Basic rule:** AI "can generate a solid draft insight in minutes" for the factors of Worksheet 2 — but "the answers you get require critical eye and fact checking".

Three recommended tools (as of the course):
- **Perplexity** — AI-powered search engine with real-time sources + clickable links, **deep-research mode**. Good for: market-size data, competitive analysis, industry trends, regulation/policies.
- **Consensus** — AI-powered **academic** search engine (peer-reviewed literature, clickable links). Good for: technical feasibility, possibly clinical benefits, economic impact, operational insights.
- **ChatGPT / generic GenAI chatbot** — a "360-degree partner" with deep-research mode. Good for: customer personas/behaviors ("synthetic users", cf. Week 3), scenario planning, synthesis of findings, creative problem solving, brainstorming.

Usage rules against hallucination/unverified data:
1. **Cross-check sources** — verify credibility, demand references/explanations.
2. **Be specific with queries** — a detailed, targeted prompt; set objectives and the chatbot's role up front; challenge the chatbot with follow-up questions or have it ask you questions.
3. **Combine insights** — use different tools for a well-rounded, evidence-backed analysis; have the LLM integrate/synthesize the data from the different sources.

## Worksheet 2B — "Where to Impact"

An extension for **double-bottom-line ventures** (financial profit **plus** positive social/environmental impact). It estimates the impact **in advance / at the outset** — "there are many frameworks for measuring the *actual* impact of social initiatives, but none to estimate the impact *beforehand*".

**Prerequisite in step 1 (Worksheet 1):** For double-bottom-line, add the **beneficiaries** to the opportunity definition. Market opportunity = Application (from core competence) × Customers **× Beneficiaries**. Beneficiaries can be: paying customers, end users, other value-chain members (employees, suppliers), parties outside the value chain (e.g. TOMS Shoes), or "the planet" (e.g. clean energy).

**Representation on the map:** Impact is marked with **heart symbols** on the sticky note: **1 heart = low, 2 = medium, 3 = high, 4 hearts = super high impact.**

**Three impact factors** (built analogously, "it works just the same"):

1. **Problem severity** — "Are you trying to address an important and painful problem for these beneficiaries?"
   > "Is this really an unresolved social or environmental concern? Does it bear significant detrimental consequences? And how big is this problem in terms of affected people, affected areas, etc.?"
   Supporting resources: **UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**, **Global Risk Report (WEF)**.

2. **Pertinent solution** — "Is your solution well suited to address this social or environmental challenge and to be adopted by your beneficiaries?"
   > "How effective and efficient is your solution? Is it simple, reliable, self-sufficient, etc.? Is it better than existing solution? And does it avoid secondary harm or negative effect?"
   Supporting tools: **Theory of Change**, **Life Cycle Assessment**.

3. **Impact reach** — "Can you achieve a broad, long-lasting impact?"
   > "How easy will it be to measure and report impact? … Will you have a long-term enduring effect? And how scalable is your solution?"
   Supporting resources (for *measuring* in operation): **SDG Indicators**, **Impact Measurement Project**.

**Takeaways:**
- Helps determine **in advance** "where to impact".
- In double-bottom-line ventures, "where to play" often means choosing **what comes first** — financial gains or social gains. The Navigator makes the trade-offs visible/confrontable, but does not say what is right/wrong.
- **Non-profit:** Worksheet 2B can be used **standalone** — or as a **revised Attractiveness Map** with **impact on the Y-axis** and Challenge on the X-axis (instead of the economic potential).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Making the scoring an end in itself.** The X on the scale is not the goal — understanding the up-/downsides is. (R4)
- **Mathematizing** (scale → 1–4, average). Avoid; stay qualitative. (R3)
- **Overweighting build challenges, neglecting go-to-market challenges.** "Delivering the offering may be much more difficult than creating it." (Factor 4)
- **Scoring from the inside-out instead of the customer's view.** Compelling reason to buy only "through the eyes of your customers" — "go out of the building". (Factor 1)
- **Falling in love with the first assessment.** These are hypotheses at first. (Validating & learning)
- **Inconsistency** in weights / time horizon / number mapping across options. (R5, R6)
- **Scoring in isolation.** Proposing scores from one opportunity's own evidence without comparing against the sheets already scored. (R7)
- **"Mid" as a fallback.** Reaching for the middle when evidence is unclear hides the fog; show it in the confidence colour instead. (R9)
- **Range restriction across the set.** When every opportunity clusters (e.g. every Potential Mid or below, every Challenge High or above), re-check calibration — horizon, anchors, a drift to the middle — before treating it as a finding. (R6, R7)
- **Ignoring / misreading missing competition.** It can be just as worrying as strong competition. (Factor 6)
- **Adopting AI output unchecked** — risk of hallucination, always cross-check; show sources per claim by default so the team can. (Using AI for market research)
- **Treating synthetic personas as evidence.** Synthetic-interview output is a directional assumption to validate with real people, never "research" and never "customers said". Name the kind of evidence: desk research (with sources), synthetic personas, or the team's own customer conversations. (Factor 1)
- **Letting a single factor decide** without the full picture — but: Compelling reason to buy is a "No-Go" condition, low = stop. (Factor 1; R5)
- **Giving up at "not attractive".** It is a valuable learning outcome, not the end. (R13)
