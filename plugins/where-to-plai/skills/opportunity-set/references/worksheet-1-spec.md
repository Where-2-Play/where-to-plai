# Worksheet 1 — Market Opportunity Set (Generating the Opportunity Set)

Source: EPFL MOOC "From lab to market" transcripts, plus the Where-to-Play book draft and cheat sheet.

---

## Purpose (in one sentence)

Worksheet 1 produces the **Market Opportunity Set** — a **broad, varied** collection of plausible market opportunities (each opportunity = **application × customer**) — by first describing your own core abilities **detached from the product** (**De-linking**) and then re-connecting them to applications and customer groups (**Re-linking**), in order to create real strategic choice and agility.

---

## Key concepts

- **De-linking:** "you are looking at your technological capabilities in their own right." The core abilities are described **in their own logic** — detached from the application or customer originally envisioned.
- **Re-linking:** "allows you to link these technical abilities to new types of market opportunities." The de-linked abilities are reconnected to **applications** and **customers**.
- **Market opportunity:** "a combination between an application and a certain customer set" — the glue that binds them is the **need**: "The link that ties both ends is the need: applications are designed to address specific needs of specific customers."
- **Application:** "a specific usage or function, which you can create with your core technologies and abilities. It can be based on some or on all of the key elements."
- **Customer / market segment:** "a set of people that share a common need and are expected to respond in a similar way to your offering."
- **Core abilities / capabilities / competencies:** used synonymously. "a unique ability can be a specific know-how, a technological element, a rare resource etc." A patent or other IP is **not** an ability: it is legal protection, not something the venture can *do* — see Rule 1.7.
- **Cognitive flexibility:** "the mental ability to develop a variety of viewpoints" — the actual goal of De-linking, so that you "allow[s] yourself to see more of the opportunity landscape" and don't become a "one-trick pony."
- **Market Opportunity Set** (the result): the selected, comparable set of options for further evaluation. "a varied set of plausible market options that creates real strategic choice."
- **Continents vs. countries** (abstraction-level metaphor): "A continent … is a broad market domain (or a vertical). And every continent contains multiple countries — or market segments." Opportunities in the set must sit at the **same level of abstraction**.

**Structure of the worksheet:** two halves. The **upper part** = abilities (De-linking). The **lower part** = applications + customers (Re-linking), drawn as a tree: application → customer segment → optional sub-segment (Rule 2.7). The grey divider line in the middle is "actually the most important bar because it separates the abilities … from where they are applied."

## Normative steps / rules (the core)

### Step 1 — De-linking: describe core abilities "in their own right" (upper part of the worksheet)

**Rule 1.1 — Decouple from the product.** Describe the abilities "in a more general way, that is, decoupled from the specific product idea or customer need that you may originally have had in mind." "They are described in their own right without an application in mind, although you might have had an application."

**Rule 1.2 — List the building blocks.** List "the key elements that in combination form your technology or unique abilities." An ability can be "an important know-how … about a specific process, a rare resource … or a special capability (such as manufacturing)." Technologies can be broken down "based on their unique functionality (such as pattern recognition …), or based on their structural design (such as a nano-sized camera)."

**Rule 1.3 — Name functions AND properties.** "describe their main properties as well as the functions they can perform." Guiding question from the definitions catalog: "describe each capability in its own right: what is its **functions / properties / limitations** etc."

**Rule 1.4 — As generic as possible.** "We urge you to think about and describe your technological elements and abilities in the most generic manner, as they will form the basis for different applications!"

**Rule 1.5 — Existing AND planned abilities allowed; AI as a "what if".** "They can be based on what you already possess or what you are planning to develop." Hypothetically adding abilities is explicitly allowed: "what would happen if I would have, for instance, AI capabilities?" — this "opens up maybe a completely new way of thinking about the opportunities."

**Rule 1.6 — Existing companies.** "if this is an existing company (i.e. not a startup), they can also mention their existing strengths (i.e. customer base / existing distribution channels / large R&D budgets etc)."

**Rule 1.7 — Patents and IP are not abilities.** "Patent is not an ability. It's a legal protection, not something the venture can *do* — better placed as a moat/assumption for WS2 Challenge, not listed as an ability in the de-linking table." Describe the function the patent protects as the ability; record the patent itself for Worksheet 2 — as an entry barrier / sustainable advantage under **External risk(s)**, or as an IP milestone in the "key milestones to implementation" homework.

**Definition criteria for "well-defined" core abilities** (verbatim from the catalog, to be adopted as normative):
- "It is important to label the competencies clearly and try to avoid general labels like ‚algorithm'."
- "don't use professional jargon (or complicated terms) — so that everyone can understand what you do."
- "for those who are worried about IP protection — you don't need to describe how the ‚technological element' works, but what it can do."
- "there is no right or wrong way of doing this task, as long as it serves the goal of enhancing the cognitive flexibility (i.e. it's a mean to an end)."
- "sometimes the list of core competencies is hard to understand if the overall context is missing … we ask users to add the context before the ‚opportunity discovery assistant' helps them identify applications and customers."

> **Note — Pitfall #4 (no right/wrong):** "there's no wrong or right in filling the upper part of worksheet one. The goal is to increase your cognitive flexibility by describing the competences in their own right."

---

### Step 2 — Re-linking: discover applications × customers (lower part of the worksheet)

**Rule 2.1 — Opportunity = application × customer, joined by the need.** "A market opportunity is any combination of application and customers … The link that ties both ends is the need."

**Rule 2.2 — Think broad and "out of the box" (both ends).** "BE OPEN, THINK UNCONSTRAINED, SHOW YOUR CREATIVITY … try to generate a wide variety of new opportunities." "Think BROAD." Deliberately look for **distant** markets too: "look for market opportunities that could, in fact, be quite distant to the market that you may have thus far envisioned."

**Rule 2.3 — Derive applications from one, several, or imagined abilities.** "This application area … might be based on one of the abilities … or on multiple ones, or even imagined ones." It is **not** necessary to fix a particular ability to a particular application: "an application can be based on some or all of your competencies." Two ways to generate applications: (a) **recombine** core elements, (b) **combine** your technology **with other technologies** to add functionality. So re-linking is **not organised per single ability**: "an opportunity can draw on some or all of the listed abilities in combination, not just one at a time". Noting which abilities an application draws on is optional.

**Rule 2.4 — Customers: who has the need?** For each application: "think about who may need these different applications." "Think as broadly as possible about who may have the need for your applications."

**Rule 2.5 — Zoom-in / zoom-out to multiply options.** "You can further segment these customer sets to identify even more opportunities!" "If you zoom out, you go to bigger segments. If you zoom in, you go to more refined segments." Zoom-in produces sub-segments; zoom-out opens the view onto neighboring segments.

**Rule 2.6 — Separate buyers from users when needed.** "in some cases the customers (i.e. the buyers) and the users are not the same. In that case it is recommended to explain who are the buyers and who are the users."

**Rule 2.7 — Draw the lower part as a tree, not a flat list.** Arrange ideas "directly as applications × the different customer sets who may need them" — "larger customer segments with sub-segments nested underneath, grouped per application. … This is how the worksheet's lower part should be drawn if sitting with the founders." For example:

- **Application 1 — private audio zone**
  - Segment 1 — Workplace: open offices, call centers, industrial plants
  - Segment 2 — Transportation: auto, airlines, …
- **Application 2 — differentiated audio zones**
  - Segment 1 — Healthcare (with relevant sub-segments)
  - Segment 2 — Education (with relevant sub-segments)

Each leaf (a segment, or a sub-segment where one exists) is one market opportunity (Rule 2.1). After screening, every leaf carries a status — **on the set / screened out / parked** — and stays in the tree, so the breadth the team explored remains visible.

**Definition criteria for "well-defined" market opportunities** (verbatim from the catalog, normative):
- "a poorly defined market opportunity is one where any of the 2 elements are not clearly stated."
- "the market segment (or sub-segment) should be clearly defined (i.e. not vague or too broad like: industrial users or private consumers). If it's defined well, one should be able to understand how you reach out or find that target audience."
- "the market opportunities that founders choose to further explore and compare … should include options on the same level of abstractions (i.e. don't compare continents to countries)."

---

### Step 3 — Right level of abstraction (continents vs. countries)

**Rule 3.1 — Not too broad, not too narrow.** "define your market opportunities so that they are not too narrow but also not too broad." Rationale: "too broad" → contains sub-segments with differing motivations → you "average" the evaluation; "too narrow" → you miss a bigger opportunity and the customer base is too small.

**Rule 3.2 — Same level for comparison.** "you want to compare … continents with continents or countries with countries." "in order to arrive at a consistent Market Opportunity Set, you will need to have opportunities that are on the same level of abstraction. Otherwise, it will be very difficult to compare them."

**Rule 3.3 — Comparability & data as the yardstick.** Choose the level so that "you actually have a meaningful compare and contrast" and "meaningful data and … structural information" are available.

> Note: the truly "right" level often only emerges through customer contact: "Sometimes founders just don't know what is the right level until they start talking to potential customers — so they refine the definition."

---

### Step 4 — Initial Screening (from broad brainstorm to set)

**Rule 4.1 — Generate broadly first, then screen.** Reduce from typically "20, 30 different opportunities" to "a more manageable size … like four or five."

**Rule 4.2 — Screening questions** (near-verbatim; the book and transcript versions largely match):
1. "Does the customer need exist?" — "Is there truly a customer need there?"
2. "Can we really satisfy the customer need using our unique abilities, and better than existing solutions?"
3. "Are there severe restrictions that would hinder us from executing on this market opportunity?" (e.g. "an external legal implication restriction")
4. "Will this opportunity be in conflict with any of our core values?" — "is this opportunity actually in line with our moral compass".

**Rule 4.3 — Set your own selection criteria explicitly.** "set your criteria for selecting opportunities for further examination … Which among these options … should deserve being promoted … to your market opportunity set."

**Rule 4.4 — Prefer heterogeneity (maximize learning).** "create a varied set of options" → "you can maximize the learning about different fields." Example FeelIT: deliberately "heterogeneous options."

**Rule 4.5 — Screen one market opportunity at a time.** "The unit of analysis for screening is always a market opportunity — one specific application for one specific segment. Screening per 'bucket/application' masks the different segments inside it and gives a misleading verdict." Screen each leaf of the tree (Rule 2.7), never an application or segment as a whole.

**Rule 4.6 — Recommend; the team decides.** An AI-assisted screening outcome is a **recommendation** with its reasoning, not a verdict or pass/fail: "same content, less of an implication that the system has decided". The team sets each leaf's status (on the set / screened out / parked).

---

### Step 5 — Outcome: the Market Opportunity Set

**Rule 5.1 — Target size "a handful" / 3–5.** "choose a handful, roughly a handful of options for initial investigation." Book: "Having 3-5 market opportunities in your Market Opportunity Set is typically a good starting point."

**Rule 5.2 — Clear naming.** "give each market opportunity a clear name." Each option as a sticky note in the designated area of the Navigator design board.

**Rule 5.3 — Iterative, "breathing" process.** "this is an iterative process. So you can add, refine, dismiss options over time … bring some back that you have not looked at." Add new options to the set only after they pass initial screening. This embodies the WtP design principle of a longitudinal/breathing process.

---

### Step 6 — Boost discovery skills (discovery tooling)

**Rule 6.1 — Don't be limited by your own prior knowledge.** "don't be bound by your own prior knowledge … think broadly about application domains." "People will primarily discover market opportunities that are associated with their own, existing knowledge."

**Rule 6.2 — Bring in external / heterogeneous sources.** "work with heterogeneous teams" and "invite … external people … from different domains with different backgrounds." "Share your underlying technology with these trusted parties and ask them … where would you apply this technology?" Precondition: describe the functionalities "in layman terms."

**Rule 6.3 — Industry/market lists & patent search.** "Plowing Lists of Industries and Markets" (hierarchical, good for zoom-in/-out) and "Perform a Patent Search" as systematic creativity starters.

**Rule 6.4 — LLM / AI as a discovery assistant.** "ask ChatGPT or any other LLM model … to help you with this brainstorming." Think "about this AI chatbot as another team member with the power of having a 360 degrees view of the world." WtP has embedded an **Opportunity Discovery Assistant** that, once you enter the core competencies + context, automatically suggests applications and customers. Tips: (1) "explain your core competencies clearly and … the context of your innovation … and its limitations"; (2) "ask for possible applications and possible target audiences that may need it"; (3) "dive deeper with further prompts to understand possible sub-segments." Caveat: "some ideas might not be relevant or even valuable, but it's still a great starting point." This is **core** for the WtP plugin (Opportunity Discovery Assistant as agent/command). Users won't ask for an agent they don't know exists, so offer it during re-linking in one plain sentence rather than waiting for a request.

**Rule 6.5 — Cultivate the Innovator's-DNA skills.** Five trainable discovery behaviors (Dyer/Gregersen/Christensen, "The Innovator's DNA"): **Associating** ("make connection across seemingly unrelated questions, problems, or ideas"), **Questioning** ("ask … why, why not, what if"), **Observing**, **Experimenting** ("adopt a mindset of hypothesis testing in the world"), **Networking** ("find and test ideas through diverse network that vary in backgrounds and perspectives"). "these five skills and behaviors can actually be cultivated."

---

### Value / output of this process (why WS1 matters)

"as you do this de-linking and re-linking process, you enhance your cognitive flexibility. You become more alert to other promising opportunities." Four benefits:
1. **Cognitive flexibility** — don't "fall in love with their initial idea," and recognize signals against the first idea.
2. **Options at hand** — pick the best option first, avoid inferior directions, stay **agile** (Plan B / growth options). "look before you leap."
3. **IP protection** — knowing further application areas lets you "better protect your invention by writing the right patents and the right claims."
4. **Clear, jargon-free communication** — explain the innovation "in a simple, clear manner" so others can see further applications/customers, and so you can win investors and collaborators.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Opportunities not cleanly defined.** "An opportunity is an application plus customer segment. These opportunities should not be too narrow, they should not be too broad." Both elements must be clearly named.
2. **Trapped by your own prior knowledge.** "don't be bound by your own prior knowledge" → bring in external/heterogeneous perspectives.
3. **Inconsistent abstraction levels.** "compare … continents with continents or countries with countries" — otherwise there's no fair comparison in Worksheet 2. "it would be unfair to say … because it's more broadly defined it's larger."
4. **Misreading the upper part as "right/wrong".** "there's no wrong or right in filling the upper part" — the goal is cognitive flexibility; feel free to put new abilities (e.g. AI) into the mix.
5. **Selection without explicit criteria.** "set your criteria for selecting opportunities for further examination."
6. **Stopping too early.** "don't give up too early" — startups have sometimes identified >50 opportunities; at the same time there are "decreasing returns to search."
7. **Abstract/context-bound technologies.** "technologies that are more abstract and … tightly coupled with a specific application context are more difficult to decouple … in their own right" — they require more effort during De-linking.
8. **Forcing an ability onto a single application.** "sometimes founders feel they need to tie a specific competence to a specific application. That is not necessary."
