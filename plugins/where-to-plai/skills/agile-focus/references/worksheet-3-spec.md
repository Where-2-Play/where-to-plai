# Worksheet 3 — Agile Focus Strategy (Step 3 of the Market Opportunity Navigator)

Source: EPFL MOOC "From lab to market" transcripts, plus the Where-to-Play book draft and cheat sheet.

---

## Purpose (in one sentence)

Worksheet 3 sets the strategic focus: it selects the **Primary Market Opportunity** (the one pursued with full force) and builds a lean portfolio of **Backup** and **Growth** options around it, preserving *focus and agility at the same time* — the result is shown on the **Agile Focus Dartboard**.

---

## Core terms

- **Agile Focus Strategy** — a strategy that deliberately balances the tension between *focus* (concentrating resources) and *flexibility* (staying agile and adaptable) by keeping selected additional market opportunities open. The output of Step 3.
- **Primary Market Opportunity** — "the option that you will pursue with full force, investing most of your resources and capabilities in conquering it."
- **Backup Option** ("Plan B") — a related, attractive opportunity that lets you *change direction* if the Primary fails. It answers: "if we are not successful with the primary market, what shall we do next?"
- **Growth Option** — a related, attractive opportunity that creates *additional value* if the Primary succeeds. It answers: "if we are successful with our primary market, what shall we do next?"
- **Relatedness** — the degree to which the resources, capabilities, and relationships of one opportunity transfer to another; two axes: **Product relatedness** and **Market relatedness**.
- **Real Options / "keeping options open"** — a small investment now buys "the right, but not the obligation, to further invest in the future."
- **Agile Focus Dartboard** — a target-board visualization: centre = *pursue now*, middle ring = *keep open*, outer area = *storage*.
- **Investment level** — *pursue now* · *keep open for later* · *place in storage*. The Primary is *pursue now* by default; *pursue now* on any other option means pursuing it in parallel with the Primary.
- **Gold Mine / Moon Shot / Quick Win / Questionable** — the four zones of the WS2 Attractiveness Map; input for the Primary choice.

---

## Normative steps / rules (the main part)

### Underlying logic: why focus AND stay open at all?

- **Market choice is fundamental** for two reasons: (1) it determines your ability to create value and generate revenue; (2) it has an **"imprinting effect"** — "it shapes the DNA of your venture in ways that are sometimes difficult to undo."
- **Uncertainty is inherent.** The Gruber/Tal study of over 300 startups found that "**73% of them changed their initial market entry decision in the first three years of existence.**" → Adaptation is the norm, not the exception.
- **Startups cannot "bet on multiple horses"** the way large corporations can (lack of resources). The challenge: "How do you focus and stay agile at the same time?" The answer is the Agile Focus Strategy.
- **Investors want both** — focus *and* maneuverability. The Reid Hoffman rule: "show focus … by emphasizing what you're really betting on. However, show some maneuverability. Don't just say that you have five different options. Instead, say that you're doing one, but you also have some fallback or maneuvering options."

### Step A — Choose the Primary Market Opportunity (top row of WS3)

**Rule: a choice is mandatory.** "you do need to make a choice. Because if you try to pursue multiple markets simultaneously, you will end up juggling with too many balls in the air." Steve Jobs: "focusing is about saying no." But the choice allows *sequential* growth — the other opportunities are not thrown away.

**Basis of the choice = the Attractiveness Map (WS2) plus its underlying evidence.** Four canonical map patterns and their decision rule:

1. **One Clearly Superior Opportunity** — one opportunity clearly in the **Gold Mine** quadrant → "making the decision is relatively easy. You will focus on the most attractive market opportunity." The rest can serve as Growth/Backup.
2. **Diagonal Trade-off** — opportunities spread along the risk/return diagonal: *Quick Wins* (low risk/low return) to *Moon Shots* (high risk/high return). No single preferred Primary. "You can start with the quick wins, you can start with the moonshot. It really depends on your tendency to take risk and the context." **Tip:** "many times quick wins can be a great starting point towards future moonshots" — use them as a *stepping stone*. The choice depends on personal preferences, risk appetite, stakeholder interests, and available/reachable resources.
3. **Options all Closely Located** — all in the same quadrant or scattered around the centre. "choosing the most attractive one is not apparent with your eyes. … go back to worksheet two" — decide based on the upsides/downsides there or on individual factors (e.g. *time to revenues*); if needed, choose the option you can *shape* into something more attractive over time.
4. **No Attractive Options** — all in **Questionable** (or borderline). "go back to the drawing board" → return to Worksheet 1 for additional opportunities, or reshape an option. **Recommendation: the Primary should not be in the Questionable zone.** Until an option qualifies, WS3 can still be used to *prioritise learning* (implementation tip 1 below).

**Additional layers above the map** (factors the map does not show) — to be checked in this order:

- **Personal fit** — "Is this option aligned with your passion, your values? You have to love it. You have to wake up every morning with energy to invest your life into this market opportunity." Keyword **"product / market / founder fit"**.
- **Fit with stakeholders' interest** — existing investors, partners, and your customer base can influence the choice (e.g. investors with a domain preference or a weighting toward *time to revenues*).
- **Resource constraints** — budget/human capital can force you to start with a *Quick Win* instead of a *Moon Shot*.

**Three decision tips:**

- "there are really no such thing as a perfect market opportunity. Options always have some upsides, but also downsides."
- **Team decision** — a critical decision; everyone must be aligned and understand the logic.
- **Take the time** — "focusing from day one is actually a myth." Allow enough time to explore.

> **Plugin note:** the team-decision tip applies to every WS3 call, not only the Primary — each candidate's role, keep open vs. storage, parallel pursuit, and (when no option can be Primary yet) what to learn next. The plugin proposes and asks; it writes only what the team decided.

**When is the right time to decide?** (three triggers):

1. **When learning reaches a point of saturation** — new information is only familiar; learning is no longer productive (some residual uncertainty may remain).
2. **When you are running out of money** ("oxygen!") — fundraising forces a clear strategy.
3. **When you have to invest in branding** — a brand name implies a market/application (example *Get Taxi*); choose a broader brand name if needed (*Amazon*).

→ **Enter the chosen Primary in the top row of WS3 and place it in the "pursue now" area (centre) of the dartboard.**

### Step B — Find candidates for Backup & Growth (columns under the Primary)

**Minimum portfolio: at least one backup option + one growth option.** Each candidate is assessed for a role — backup, growth, or both (Step D).

**Where candidates come from:**

- Primarily: other opportunities from the existing set / the Attractiveness Map.
- Additionally: opportunities that were *not originally in the set* but look interesting once the Primary is fixed, because they are related. **Rule:** such new opportunities must be *added to the Market Opportunity Set and re-assessed in WS2* (mark them in the dartboard with a colour for "not yet assessed").

**Important — the number of columns is not binding:** "you don't necessarily have to assess three candidates … The fact that the worksheet has three columns is meaningless. You can assess less or more. Just add another worksheet if you need."

### Step C — Assess Relatedness (two axes, each on a 3-level scale)

**Definition:** "relatedness of two market opportunities means that you can effectively leverage the resources, the capabilities, the relationship that you are developing for one opportunity. And with that, you can succeed in the other option." **Consequence:** "the more related the two market opportunities are, the less effort will be required to keep it open. And also the easier it will be to make the change."

> **Note:** Entrepreneurs usually check Product relatedness but **neglect Market relatedness** — "This may turn out to be a critical mistake." Both axes are equally important.

**Scale (identical for both axes, qualitative):**

- Circles do not overlap → **not related**
- Circles overlap somewhat → **somewhat related**
- Circles overlap strongly → **tightly related**

**1) Product Relatedness** — "the extent to which the development of the two products require similar resources and capabilities." Guiding questions — *To what extent do the products share…*:

- "**Technological competences?** (i.e., the functions and features of the products rely on similar technological developments)"
- "**Required resources?** (i.e., employees, manufacturing equipment, intellectual property etc.)"
- "**Necessary networks?** (i.e., suppliers, partners, or other members of the value chain)"

**2) Market Relatedness** — "the extent to which the marketing and distribution of the two products require similar resources and capabilities." Guiding questions — *To what extent do the customers share…*:

- "**Values and benefits?** (i.e., you can leverage your brand and reputation from one market to the other)"
- "**Sales channels?** (i.e., you can utilize the same distribution channels for both markets)"
- "**Word of mouth?** (i.e., satisfied customers in one market can promote your product in the other)"

→ From the three answers on each axis, derive its overall value (not / somewhat / tightly) and enter it in WS3.

### Step D — Check risk balancing (backup-specific)

**Rule (Backup):** a good backup option is *related* but must **not share the same major risks / showstoppers / core assumptions** with the Primary. "your plan B should not share the same major risk with your primary market opportunity or rely on the same major assumptions" (e.g. an expected technological standard). The goal: "you want to be able to succeed in your backup option, even if you have failed in your primary market."

- Method: use WS2 evidence to identify the Primary's major risks/showstoppers and compare them with the candidates'. Example: if the Primary depends heavily on regulation, look for a backup *without* that dependency.

**Rule (Growth):** as **attractive** as possible (high potential, low challenge) **and** as **tightly related** as possible — "so we can stand on our own shoulders as we grow."

**One option can be Backup AND Growth at once:** "some opportunities can serve both as your backup option and as your growth option." Condition: attractive + highly related, but without shared major risks. Then one option suffices instead of two.

**Be aware of two trade-offs in the selection:**

- **Attractiveness vs. relatedness** — more attractive options are often less related (and vice versa).
- **Relatedness vs. risk balancing** — highly related options often share *similar* risks; more *distant* options spread risk better (which is exactly what you want for a backup).

### Step E — Set the investment level (bottom row of WS3)

Three levels — "three levels of investment":

1. **Keep open (default)** — "allocate little resources and attention, maybe 5%, 10% of your resources to keep these opportunities breathing." Concretely: (a) **stay informed** in the market; (b) **build flexible resources & capabilities** (modular technology, broader IP/patent protection with future claims); (c) **develop an appropriate identity / brand** that does not lock you out. "make the firm more robust to change but without compromising on the pursuit of your primary."
2. **Pursue now — in parallel** — pursue an additional opportunity *simultaneously* with the Primary. "quite a dangerous strategy for very early stage startups." **Only sensible under TWO conditions:** (1) "your primary market opportunity is really highly uncertain" (high risk), **and** (2) "the opportunities are tightly related, both in terms of the product and the market." (In the book: "How critical?" + "How related?")
3. **Place in storage** — "you do nothing about it for now. You only keep them in the back of your mind." They may become relevant later (your own pursuit or licensing). "you don't pay rent for putting them in storage" — they can be stored indefinitely.

→ Mark the decision for each option in the bottom row of WS3. The Primary is *pursue now* by default and needs no candidate column of its own; *pursue now* marked on a candidate column means pursuing that option in parallel with the Primary. Every kept-open option names its role — backup, growth, or both (both only when it meets both conditions in Step D).

### Step F — Fill in the Agile Focus Dartboard (output)

- **Centre / "pursue now":** the Primary Market Opportunity (and possibly one option pursued in parallel — "If you decide to pursue two options in parallel, put them both at the center").
- **Middle ring / "keep open":** the chosen Backup and Growth options, each labelled with its role.
- **Outer / "storage":** all remaining opportunities.
- **Highlight** new options not yet assessed in WS2 with a colour.

### Implications & mindset

The foresight from the Agile Focus Strategy shapes the venture from day 1: (a) **resources & capabilities** (modular technology, a broader IP net, human capital, stakeholder network); (b) **identity / culture / structure** (hard to undo — design deliberately); (c) **branding & marketing** (a brand name that does not lock you out — counter-examples GetTaxi/Salesforce/CDNow); (d) **fundraising** (investor type, risk mitigation à la Reid Hoffman, a signal of a serious team).

**Three implementation tips:** (1) only finalize the strategy once you trust the WS2 assessment/map — before that, use WS3 to *prioritize learning*; (2) it is a **dynamic tool** — update it continuously, add/discard options, make the learning path visible; (3) it is **"more than a strategy — it's a mindset"**: it also builds your own *cognitive flexibility* / alertness.

## Common pitfalls

- **Pursuing too many opportunities in parallel** ("candy store" / "too many balls in the air") — leads to paralysis. A choice is mandatory.
- **Ignoring market relatedness** and checking only product relatedness — a critical mistake, since distant markets become unaffordable for a small venture.
- **Choosing a backup with a shared major risk** — then it is not a real Plan B.
- **Keeping an option open without a role** — kept open *as what*? Each kept-open option is a backup, a growth option, or both.
- **Parallel pursuit** without the two conditions (high uncertainty + tightly related) — dangerous for early startups.
- **A Primary in the Questionable zone** — avoid if possible; rather go back to WS1.
- **Lock-in through brand name / identity** (GetTaxi → Get) — expensive to undo.
- **Premature scaling / focusing too early** — "focusing from day one is a myth"; per Startup Genome, 74% fail due to premature scaling.
- **Agile focusing ≠ de-focusing** — the focus is deliberately planned a bit broader, but ends up clearly defined; "the most difficult part in focusing is deciding what not to do."

- **Inergio** (MOOC example): Primary = traffic management systems (Gold Mine); keep open = transportable monitoring (growth), oil & gas remote monitoring (backup+growth), RVs (future growth); storage = data-center backup power.
- **biaWear / BeAware** (MOOC, baseball wearable): Primary = competitive baseball parents (Gold Mine); recreational parents (growth, keep open), baseball gyms B2B (backup, keep open), adult weekend warriors (growth → storage).
- **Augury** (main book example): Primary = HVAC commercial buildings; backup = manufacturing machines; growth = residential HVAC + elevators.
- **KalOptics** (book): "closely located" pattern; Primary = film studios; growth/backup = animation/gaming, designers, advertisers.
- **Camero** (parallel-strategy example): military + law enforcement in parallel (tightly related, military highly uncertain).
- **"Navigator's Navigator"** (Gruber/Tal on themselves, epilogue): Primary = startups; parallel = educators; keep open = TTOs + investors (growth), established firms (backup).
